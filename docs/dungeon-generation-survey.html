Dungeon generation: where things stand

A survey before the redesign. 12 November 2026.

The short version

  • The generator in the game today is the sixth design across two projects. All six build a layout first and decide what goes in it afterwards. None of them ever knew what a room was for.
  • A floor today is about 14 rooms joined by a web of corridors with about 32 loops in it. On three floors in four there's no room a party has to pass through. The direct way down is about 41 steps; walking the whole floor is about 700.
  • That shape is why traps, keys and puzzles have never fitted. There are no chokepoints to guard, almost no dead ends to hide things in, and no order in which a party is known to reach things.
  • Neither project's history shows a pit, a teleporter, a switch or a secret door ever being placed. Traps on the floor appear twice in the old project, placed at random or by nearness, and were turned off both times. Further trap trials on a lost branch had the same faults.
  • The generator has real faults as it stands. About one floor in 130 comes out with the way down walled off. The boss lands behind the locked door on two floors in three and somewhere else on the rest. The same seed doesn't give the same floor twice.
  • The written goals from 2025 say this game is not a puzzle box. What you're asking for now includes puzzles. That's the first thing to settle.

How this was done

I read the current generator and everything that uses its output, the design docs in this repo, and the whole of drpg2, the TypeScript project this game was ported from: its five dungeon documents, its archived design, both of its generators and its commit history. Then I wrote tests/measure_floors.tscn, which generates floors and counts what's in them. The numbers below come from 2,000 floors: 1,500 at the normal 31×31 size and 500 at the great floor's 41×41.

Run it with godot --headless --path . tests/measure_floors.tscn -- runs 200 maps 2. It prints a text map of any floor whose stairs can't be reached.

The work you remember on making floors more like Wizardry's isn't in this repo. It happened in drpg2 between August and November 2025. This game began in January 2026 as a port of where that ended up.

What the generator does today

  1. Tries 80 times to drop a room of 3, 5 or 7 tiles a side at a random spot, keeping the ones that don't overlap.
  2. Fills every remaining gap with one-wide maze corridor.
  3. Joins rooms and mazes wherever they touch, then opens one in five of the remaining touching points as well.
  4. Removes every corridor dead end, repeatedly, until none is left.
  5. Puts a door on most openings between a room and a corridor.
  6. Puts the stairs up in the middle of the first room it made, and the stairs down in the room farthest away in a straight line.
  7. Picks one opening of the stairs-down room at random, locks it, and walls up the others.
  8. Makes each room an encounter zone, and all the corridors one more.
  9. On a boss floor, marks up to four tiles three to six steps from the stairs down as the boss's zone. Those steps are counted through walls.
  10. From floor 3, puts an inscription or a shrine in the corner of a random room.

Rooms carry an id, a position and a size. Nothing records what a room is for, what's in it, or what a party must have done before reaching it.

Room sizes come out as 3×5 a third of the time, 5×5 and 5×7 about a fifth each, 3×7 and 3×3 about an eighth each, and 7×7 rarely. There's no room larger than 7×7 and none that isn't a rectangle.

Nearly half of every map is solid rock. That's built into the method. Walls are a full tile thick, so rooms and corridors sit on every other row and column of the map, two rooms can never share a wall, and every corridor costs a row of rock on each side. A 31×31 map is really a 15×15 lattice of places. In Wizardry a wall is a line between two squares, every square of a 20×20 floor is in use, and rooms sit back to back with a door between them. I think that's a large part of why its floors feel full and these feel like rooms strung along corridors. (The comparison is from memory of Proving Grounds, not from a measurement.)

What's wrong with it as it stands

Some floors can't be finished

15 of 2,000 floors had no way to the stairs down. That's about one in 130. A set has 33 floors, so roughly one set in four or five would have such a floor in it. On a guardian's floor that seals the dungeon below it for good; on a master's floor the dungeon can never collapse.

The cause is step 7. The generator keeps one opening of the stairs room at random and walls up the rest, without checking that the one it kept is on the side the party arrives from. Then it prunes the corridors it has orphaned. Sometimes what's left outside the locked door is a pocket joined to nothing. Nothing checks the floor afterwards.

The same step cuts off other parts of the floor on 29 of 2,000 floors, once as much as a quarter of the map. Twenty shrines or inscriptions in 3,000 landed where no one can reach them.

The boss isn't in a fixed place

The boss's zone is four tiles "three to six steps" from the stairs down, with the steps counted through walls. The stairs room varies from 3×3 to 7×7, so:

  • on 66% of boss floors the whole zone is inside the stairs room, behind the locked door;
  • on 23% it straddles the door, some tiles inside and some out;
  • on 11% it's entirely outside, in a corridor or the next room.

On one boss floor in ten, a tile of the zone is more than six real steps from the stairs. The worst was 73. The boss then appears on one of the zone's tiles at random.

The locked door has no key

Every floor's stairs room has one locked door. A thief can try to pick it as often as they like, at no cost. Without a thief the party needs a Dungeon Key, which has a 20% chance of turning up in a chest after a fight. Nothing on the floor holds a key and nothing tells the player where to look, so a party without a thief gets through when luck allows.

Other things

  • The same seed gives a different floor each time. One step shuffles with the engine's global random numbers. All 160 pairs I compared differed. Saved floors aren't affected, since a floor is stored whole once it's made, but a bad floor can't be reproduced from its seed.
  • Distance is measured in a straight line. The stairs down go in the room farthest from the stairs up as the crow flies, and rooms are graded quiet, normal or busy the same way. Walking distance isn't considered, so the way down ranges from 20 steps to 97.
  • The stairs up can be anywhere. They go in whichever room was made first. They're within three tiles of the map's edge on one floor in eight and deep in the middle on one in five.
  • Doors are uneven. About one opening in six has no door, as a side effect of how doors are shared between neighbouring rooms.
  • Shrines and inscriptions land anywhere. About 15% sit on the direct route and about a third are fifteen steps or more off it. Nothing decides which.

Why traps, keys and puzzles have never fitted

These are the findings I'd weigh most, because a new generator that shares them will have the same trouble.

The layout is made before anything knows what it's for

Every version has worked the same way: carve the floor, then look for places to put things. The stairs, the locked door, the boss, the zones and the shrines are each placed by a separate rule that looks at a finished layout and picks a spot by distance or at random. When the layout doesn't suit, the rule patches it, by walling up openings and pruning corridors. The patches are where floors break.

A puzzle can't be added this way. A switch that opens a door somewhere else needs the generator to know that the switch can be reached first, that the door stands between the party and something worth having, and that there's no other way round. None of that can be read off a finished maze. It has to be decided before the maze is drawn.

The layout is too open to gate

With 32 loops and more than three ways into the average room, there's almost always another way round. That's why the generator has to wall up the stairs room to make its one locked door mean anything. It's also why trap placement can't come out well. In a web of loops a trapped tile is either one of several routes, and so easy to avoid once known, or it's in a one-wide corridor and can't be avoided at all. The generator has no control over which.

There's nowhere to hide anything

Dead-end corridors are all removed, and a floor has one or two rooms with a single way in, one of which is the stairs room. On half the floors it's the only one. A treasure room, a secret, a key or an optional fight wants a place off the route that a player chooses to go to. The current floors have almost none.

Nothing checks the result

No version in either project has ever tested that a floor can be finished. The Gaiden IV design called for it and for generating a hundred floors to verify them, and neither was built.

What a new generator has to live with

These are the parts of the game that read a floor. Most are in good shape and none of them cares how the floor was made.

  • The tile model already has thin walls. Each tile carries a wall type on each of its four edges, and doors are edges. Movement, enemy pathfinding, line of sight, the map screen and the 3D view all read edges. A floor with rooms that share a wall would work today without changing any of them.
  • Floors are saved whole. A floor is generated once and stored with every tile. A new generator affects only floors not yet visited. Anything with state, such as a switch that's been thrown, needs a place in that stored floor.
  • Several parties share a floor. One party can throw a switch or spring a trap and another can arrive later. Rescue parties have to reach where the first one fell.
  • Aging is tuned to the current size. Deep time was measured against floors of about 520 tiles at 120 steps a day. Today the way down costs a third of a day and seeing everything costs about six. A floor that takes twice as long to explore ages a party twice as much, unless the clock is retuned with it.
  • Enemies need zones. The enemy manager fills each zone with groups and brings them back on a timer. It expects rooms as zones, the corridors as one zone, safe ground round the stairs and a boss zone.
  • The stairs seal and the collapse need a boss on floors 2, 4, 6 and 8, and a known place for it.
  • Themes and decoration are applied after the floor is made, from the dungeon's theme. They only need tiles and walls.
  • The great floor is the same generator at 41×41, and the elevator is waiting on this work.
  • The simulators (measure_deep_time, the exploration simulator) walk generated floors and will need rerunning.

Questions for the goals conversation

  1. How much of a puzzle box? The 2025 design ruled it out and you're now asking for it. It would help to know what changed, and where the line is: a lever that opens a gate two rooms away, or a floor that is one large teleporter maze.
  2. What is a party doing on a floor, besides finding the stairs? The way down is 6% of the floor today and nothing else on it has to be done. Since steps cost years, a player has good reason to skip the rest. What should make the rest worth the time?
  3. How long should a floor take? Both to get through and to see all of. This sets the size, and it moves the aging numbers with it.
  4. Are any pieces made by hand? Wholly generated floors, or hand-made rooms, set pieces and puzzle patterns that a generator arranges. This decides more about the method than anything else.
  5. Should floors differ in kind? Wizardry's do: one floor is dark, one is teleporters, one is a single long corridor. Today every floor is the same recipe with a different skin.
  6. What are traps for? A tax on carelessness, something for the thief to do, or a way of moving the party somewhere it didn't mean to go. Each one wants different placement.
  7. What should fights be tied to? Today 25 groups wander the whole floor and come back. Wizardry ties its best fights and their chests to rooms.
  8. How should a floor behave with several parties? Whether a solved puzzle stays solved for the next party, and what a rescue party has to get through.
  9. Thin walls? The game can already draw and walk them. They'd roughly double what fits on a map of the same size.

Decisions so far

From the goals conversation, 12 November 2026.

  • Puzzles are in. The 2025 decision to drop them is reversed.
  • Getting lost isn't a threat this game relies on. The mini-map and the full map stay. Spinners and teleporter mazes, whose only effect is confusion, are out.
  • A teleporter or a pit moves the party somewhere it hasn't mapped, and it may cut the party off from the way it came. The way home being a real unknown is the threat, and it isn't to be softened.
  • A way home always exists. The player doesn't know it, but the generator guarantees it. It may cross floors: down a stair, across, and up another to where the path home is clear.
  • Traps that move the party are permanent fixtures. Traps that only do damage or cause an ailment may sometimes be switched off, by a switch or some similar condition.
  • No guardian or master may be skipped. Being dropped unexpectedly into a guardian's chamber is fine and could be a high point. Arriving past one is not.
  • Everything stays inside its two-floor tier. The seals divide a dungeon into tiers: floors 1–2, 3–4, 5–6 and 7–8. The stairs behind a guardian are the only way from one tier into the next. Pits exist on floors 1, 3, 5 and 7 and drop one floor, to the near side of the guardian below. Teleporters and extra stairs join the two floors of a tier and never leave it. A pit may land in a guardian's chamber as a rare deliberate piece, and nothing lands past a guardian, alive or dead.
  • Four things reward exploring, and risk against reward is a large part of the appeal. A lever that opens a shortcut and high-value treasure are the strong pulls. A key or lever that's required to go on is used sparingly, since long backtracking to find one is frustrating. Knowledge that fleshes out the world turns up often enough to be met in ordinary play, and not so often that players click through it.
  • Age is what exploring costs. An efficient player, taking the required route and fighting only what's needed to keep level, gets a human rolled at 15–18 through all four dungeons and the fifth: about 10 to 12 years a dungeon. A player who explores everything gets two or three dungeons from a human, as the deep-time design says. The gap between the two is what side paths cost, and a shortcut is the reward that pays some of it back. To be tuned as needed.
  • Wandering enemies are easy to find and possible to avoid. There are enough that a party looking for a fight finds one without trouble, and few enough that a party can skirt round some of them and needn't fight every group it sees to get through a floor. An efficient player still has to grind a little, and deeper floors pay more experience for it. That's one of the player's tradeoffs.
  • Today's number of wandering groups is about right: one group for every 21 tiles or so, about 25 on a floor of today's size.
  • A floor is mostly rooms opening onto rooms, with short passages where they're needed, and now and then a more open space. Rooms set apart and joined by corridors, as now, can be part of the mix. This means thin walls, which the game can already draw and walk.
  • Puzzles come from hand-made templates that the generator picks, arranges and dresses. It's fine for a player to recognise one. There should be enough templates that a player doesn't meet the same one too many times in a playthrough of all five dungeons.
  • A solved puzzle stays solved for every party that comes after, until the dungeon collapses. Levers and gates stay as they were left, and hidden doors and known traps stay on the map.
  • The best loot has a place. It sits behind things like hidden doors and placed battles, which stay in one room and guard something. Wanderers still drop loot, some of it good, but not the best.
  • The guardian has a chamber of its own in front of the stairs out of the tier, in the same place relative to the stairs every time. (Follows from the above and from the survey's finding; not separately discussed.)
  • Thieves, ninjas and bards detect traps, as they do hidden doors, from one or two tiles off. The warning is vague at low skill ("something's wrong here") and exact at higher skill ("a trap on the tile to the south"). At higher skill still they can tell what kind of trap it is.
  • Each dungeon leans one way. Its theme has an affinity (melee, magic, traps, undead and the like) so that a certain kind of character shines there. It leans and doesn't lock: the right character makes a dungeon noticeably easier, and no dungeon is closed to a party without one.
  • Floors are built from hand-made blocks. The generator plans the tier first: the order of its areas and where the gate, the vaults and the guardian go. Then it picks blocks that fit those roles. A block has slots ("a trap can go here, a chest there, a guard there") and the generator decides which are filled.
  • The block library is as large as a good result needs. Claude drafts blocks, they go in, and they're revised as needed.
  • The elevator stops at the top of each tier (floors 1, 3, 5 and 7). A stop opens when the guardian above it falls, so it only reaches tiers already visited. It exists mostly to save the player's time on a trip back to town and down again. Unlike the classic elevator, it doesn't take a party anywhere new.
  • The last dungeon lines up the four affinities. The party goes through a small version of each of the four dungeons again before it reaches the Hollow King. The shape is open: four wings round a centre, or one long narrow floor that makes the party push through each in turn.
