# Campaigns: a guild that outlives its heroes

A proposal for turning the game into a series of campaigns that one guild plays through. It builds on [deep-time.md](deep-time.md), which made aging the cost of going deep, and on the racial strategies in [racial-balance.md](racial-balance.md).

## The pitch

Today the game is one campaign: a charter, eight floors, Vrakthorne at the bottom, a victory screen, and then the guild keeps delving the same dungeon with nothing new to aim for. This proposal makes that campaign the first of several. Each campaign is a new charter against a new dungeon and a new master at its bottom. The guild carries over: the roster with its levels, gear, marks and bonds, the retirees in their town posts, the Legends, and the saga.

An average character lasts two to four campaigns. That number comes out of race choice, and it's meant to differ a lot from race to race:

- **Short-lived races** (Rawulf, Mook, Lizman, Felpurr) level fastest in their best classes and peak early. They see about two campaigns before age takes them.
- **Humans** are the middle, at two or three campaigns.
- **Long-lived races** (Hobbit, Faerie, Dracon, Dwarf, Gnome, Elf) start slower but can outlive everyone and keep getting stronger the whole time. An elf could anchor every campaign the guild ever runs, but only if the dungeon doesn't kill them first.

That's the trade the player makes at character creation, and multiple campaigns are what make it pay off. In a single campaign the sprinters simply win. Over four, the player who built around a dwarf and an elf, and kept them alive, has something no sprinter roster can match.

## What a campaign is

A campaign stays what it is today: a charter from the town council, a dungeon of eight floors with guardians on 2, 4 and 6, a master on floor 8, and a victory screen when the master falls. It runs about 10 to 15 hours. The deep-time table applies inside each campaign as it does now.

After the victory screen the player can keep delving, as now, or take the **next charter** from the town. That's a deliberate choice, never forced, so a player can finish raising an apprentice or grinding a level before moving on.

## Between campaigns: the interlude

Taking the next charter skips years on the surface, a few years rolled per interlude ("Six years later, word comes up from the old mine road..."). Everyone ages through it, including the benched and the retired. A short card, like the homecoming report, shows who aged, who crossed into a new phase, and who died of old age in the meantime. Retirees can die in the interlude too, which hands their post to the next qualified retiree or leaves it empty.

The interlude is where short-lived races pay even when they sit out. It's also the natural moment for the town to change a little: a new recruit or two shows up at the guild hall, and the council's charter names the new threat.

## What carries over

- **The roster**: levels, equipment, spells, marks, bonds, ages. Dead characters stay dead (and raisable if they aren't LOST).
- **The town**: retirement posts, Legends, the guild's gold and stored items.
- **The saga**: the victory screen's saga covers every campaign so far, and the guild hall gets a chronicle of past campaigns.

What doesn't carry over is the old dungeon. Its maps, seals and bosses belong to the old charter. The new charter is a new dungeon with its own seed.

## The new dungeon

Each campaign is harder than the last, because the guild's veterans keep their levels. The simplest version that holds up:

- **A new master per campaign.** Each charter ends with a different final boss, with its own art and fight. That's the one piece of new content each campaign really needs, and it gives the campaign its name.
- **Tiered monsters instead of a new bestiary.** The existing monsters come back in stronger tiers in later campaigns (higher level, more HP, harder hits, better loot), with a tier prefix in the name and a palette shift on the existing art. That avoids needing new art for every monster in every campaign.
- **The tier grows with depth.** Floor 1 of a later campaign is only a little harder than floor 1 of the first, and floor 8 carries the full tier. A fresh recruit can still start at the top, and the deep floors still need the veterans. Apprentices remain the main way to bring a new character up to speed.

Difficulty should be tuned against a typical roster for that campaign (a few veterans, a few mid-career characters, an apprentice or two), not against the guild's strongest character. A long-lived character who has survived three campaigns should feel overpowered on the upper floors. That's the payoff.

## Things that work against the race trade-off today

Building toward this turned up three places where the current numbers push the wrong way.

**The level cap.** Levels stop at 20, and the first campaign ends around 10 to 12. A second campaign takes a survivor to the high teens, and after that the long-lived characters stop growing just when they should be pulling ahead. Wizardry never capped levels. Past level 13 each level cost the same fixed amount of XP. I'd do the same: extend the table past 20 with a flat step per level, with HP and stat gains tapering off, so an elf in their fourth campaign is still getting stronger, just slowly.

**The Youth bonus.** Youth lasts for the first 7% of a race's working life, which is about 3 years for a Rawulf, 4 for a human, and 40 for an elf. Since everyone ages the same years per campaign, an elf keeps the +25% XP bonus for about two campaigns while a Rawulf loses it partway through the first. That's backward for this design. I'd give Youth a fixed length in years (say the first five), so the bonus helps every race's first campaign equally and short-lived races keep their speed advantage from the class modifiers.

**Death is cheap for the long-lived.** Raising the dead costs years, and a few years mean almost nothing to an elf. So the risk you want long-lived characters to carry, being killed in the dungeon, barely exists unless they end up as ashes. I'd bring back Wizardry's resurrection risk: an ordinary raise can fail and leave ashes, with the chance growing each time a character has been raised and as their vitality wears down. Then the guild's 300-year-old anchor is something to protect, and losing them for good is a real possibility.

## Tuning the clock

For a Rawulf to make about two campaigns, one campaign plus its interlude needs to cost an active character roughly 15 to 18 years. A Rawulf has about 32 usable years between starting out and old age. At that rate a human makes about three campaigns, a hobbit about five, and dwarves, gnomes and elves many more. My earlier estimate of the current deep-time table is about 20 years per campaign before any interlude, which is a little high. Characters who sit out expeditions last longer, and resurrections and long grinds on the deep floors make them last less, so these are averages for someone who goes on every trip.

This needs a measured campaign, not my estimate. The campaign sim should report years aged per character per campaign before any of these numbers change.

## Build order

1. **The next charter and the interlude.** After victory, the town offers the next charter. Taking it skips surface years, shows the interlude card, and starts a new dungeon with a new seed, reusing the existing monsters at a higher tier. This alone makes campaigns repeatable.
2. **Room to grow and room to die.** Extend the XP table past 20, give Youth a fixed length, and add resurrection risk. Measure years per campaign and tune deep time and the interlude.
3. **Tiers and masters.** Tier names, palettes and loot, and a new final boss for each campaign. The bosses are the part that needs new art.
4. **The chronicle.** The saga spans campaigns, and the guild hall gets a record of past charters and Legends.

## Open questions

- Is there a last campaign? A fixed arc (say four charters, ending somewhere special) gives the game an ending. Endless charters suit a guild-management game that never quite ends. Both could work, with endless charters after the arc.
- How long are interludes? A few years each keeps the pressure on short-lived races without making benching pointless.
- Does gold carry over in full? Carrying everything is simplest. A tithe or rebuild cost would make each charter feel like a fresh start, but it may just be annoying.
- Does the old dungeon stay open after a new charter? Closing it is simpler and keeps each campaign's deep-time numbers clean.
